That Mysterious Flow of Time
We experience time flowing relentlessly onward — from the unalterable past through the fleeting present to the unknown future. Yet to physicists, that is only an illusion.
Paul Davies


Gather ye rosebuds while ye may, / Old Time is still a-flying. With this verse the English poet Robert Herrick expressed in the 17th century the common conviction that time passes. Who could doubt it? The passing of time is arguably the most basic component of human perception; we experience it more intimately than, say, the experience of space or mass. The passage of time has been compared to the flight of an arrow or to a streaming river that carries us unrelentingly from the past toward the future. Shakespeare wrote of the "whirligig of time"; his countryman Andrew Marvell of "time's winged chariot hurrying near".

As appealing as these images may be, they run into a deep and irresolvable contradiction. Nothing in known physics corresponds to the passage of time. On the contrary: physicists emphasize that time does not flow at all; it simply exists. Some philosophers maintain that the very notion of a passage is meaningless in itself, and that talk of the river of time rests on a misconception. But how can something so fundamental to our experience of the world turn out to be an error? Or is there a crucial property of time that science has so far missed?

The "now" as a mere point of view

In everyday life we divide time into past, present, and future. The grammatical structure of language reflects this fundamental distinction. We grant reality only to the present moment. We think of the past as no longer existing, the future as something shadowy to come, something without fully formed details. In this simple picture, the "now" of our conscious perception glides steadily forward, transforming events that once lay in the unformed future into the concrete but fleeting reality of the present, and banishing them from there into the fixed past.

This account appears self-evident to us, but it contradicts modern physics. Albert Einstein wrote to a friend: "Past, present, and future are only illusions, if stubborn ones." The startling statement follows directly from Einstein's special theory of relativity, which denies the present moment any absolute, universal meaning. According to the theory, simultaneity is a relative concept. Two events that occur at the same instant as observed from one reference frame can occur at different times as seen from another reference frame.

There is no unambiguous answer to the innocent question "What is happening on Mars right now?" The reason is that Earth and Mars lie far apart — about twenty light-minutes. Since no information can travel faster than light, a terrestrial observer cannot say what is happening on Mars at this very instant. He must infer the answer afterwards, once the light has traversed the distance between the planets. The indirectly inferred past event will turn out differently depending on how fast the observer moves.

For example, during a future crewed Mars mission, someone in the terrestrial mission control might ask: "What is Commander Jones doing on Base Alpha right now?" A glance at the big display board would tell his colleague that it is 12:00 on Mars, and he would answer: "Having lunch." But an astronaut passing the Earth at nearly the speed of light at the same moment would — depending on his direction of flight — look at his clock and say that it is earlier or later than 12:00 on Mars, and answer accordingly: "Making lunch" or "Washing up."

How time does not pass

Differences of this kind thwart any attempt to give the present moment special meaning, for whose "now" does this moment refer to? If you and I move relative to each other, an event that for me still lies in the undecided future may for you already belong to the fixed past.

The most natural conclusion is that both past and future are fixed once and for all. For this reason physicists prefer to picture time differently: as a time map — analogous to a land map — on which past and future are completely and jointly fixed. This notion is sometimes called block time. It lacks any reference to a special instant as the present, or to a process that systematically turns future events first into present and then into past events. In short, the time of the physicists does not pass and does not flow.

Some philosophers too have reached the same conclusion when they examined what we ordinarily mean by the passing of time. They claim the notion is self-contradictory. The concept of flowing, after all, has to do with motion. It is sensible to speak of the motion of a physical object — say, of an arrow through space — because one can observe how its position varies with time. But what meaning can be assigned to the motion of time itself? Relative to what does it move? While the ordinary kind of motion relates one physical process to another, the alleged flow of time relates time to itself. Already the simple question "How fast does time pass?" reveals the absurdity of the idea. The trivial answer "At one second per second" says nothing at all.

Although we have grown accustomed to speaking of the passage of time in daily life, this notion conveys no additional information. Consider, for example, the following text: "Alice hoped for a white Christmas, but when the day came she was disappointed, for it only rained; yet she was happy when it snowed the next day."

Although this description brims with time references and allusions to the passage of time, exactly the same information gets conveyed if one simply assigns dates to Alice's mental states — without the slightest reference to flowing time or a changing world. The following pedantic and dry catalog of facts is entirely sufficient:

- December 24: Alice hopes for a white Christmas.

- December 25: There is rain. Alice is disappointed.

- December 26: There is snow. Alice is happy.

In this description nothing happens and nothing changes. There are simply states of the world on different days, with Alice's mental states assigned to them.

Ancient Greek thinkers like Parmenides and Zeno argued similarly. A century ago, the British philosopher John McTaggart tried to draw a clear distinction between two descriptions of the world: the so-called A-series presents the world in the form of events; the B-series formulates states of the world. Each appears to be a valid description of reality, and yet they apparently contradict each other. Thus the event "Alice is disappointed" once lay in the future, then in the present, and finally in the past. But past, present, and future are mutually exclusive categories — how can one and the same event belong to all three? McTaggart used the incompatibility of the A- and the B-series as an argument for the unreality of time itself — perhaps an overly radical conclusion. Most physicists would infer less dramatically: the flow of time is unreal, but time itself is as real as space.

The standing arrow of time

Great confusion in discussions about the transience of time arises in connection with the so-called arrow of time. To deny that time flows is not to claim that the labels "past" and "future" have no physical basis. The events of the world unquestionably form a directed sequence. For example, a raw egg dropped onto the floor will shatter, while the reverse process — a broken egg spontaneously assembling itself into an intact egg — has never been observed. This irreversibility expresses itself physically in the second law of thermodynamics, according to which, in a closed system, entropy — roughly, the measure of disorder — tends to increase with time. An intact egg has lower entropy than a destroyed one.

Because most processes in nature are such irreversible physical processes, the second law produces a striking asymmetry between past and future. The time axis thereby gains a direction, the so-called arrow of time, which points from the past into the future. But that does not mean the arrow moves toward the future — any more than a compass needle pointing north indicates that the compass travels northward. Both arrows symbolize an asymmetry, not a motion.

The arrow of time denotes an asymmetry of the world with respect to time, not an asymmetry or a flowing of time itself. The labels "past" and "future" apply to temporal directions, exactly as "upward" and "downward" apply to directions in space. By contrast, speaking of "the past" or "the future" is, strictly speaking, as meaningless as saying an object rises into "the upward" or sinks into "the downward".

To illustrate the distinction between "past" and "future" on the one hand and "the past" and "the future" on the other, imagine a film that records an egg falling to the floor and shattering. If the film runs backward through the projector, every viewer immediately recognizes the unreality of the scene. Now suppose someone cuts the filmstrip into individual frames, shuffles them completely at random, and stacks them up. We will surely find it easy to put the stack into the right order, with the broken egg on top and the intact one at the very bottom. This vertical stack reproduces the asymmetry expressed by the arrow of time, for before us lies an ordered sequence along the spatial vertical — which proves that the temporal asymmetry is in fact a property of the states of the world and not a peculiarity of time itself. The film does not need to run in the cinema at all for the arrow of time to become recognizable.

Since most physical and philosophical analyses of time find no trace of a passage, we face a riddle: where does the strong, ubiquitous impression come from that the world is unceasingly in flux? Some researchers, in particular the Belgian Nobel laureate in chemistry Ilya Prigogine, now teaching at the University of Texas at Austin, hold that the subtle physics of irreversible processes makes the stream of time an objective feature of the world. But I and others believe that it is a specific illusion.

Ultimately, we do not really observe the passing of time. What we actually experience is that later states of the world differ from earlier ones that we still remember. The fact that we remember the past and not the future does not mean time passes, but that it is asymmetric. Only a curious observer registers the flow of time. A clock measures the span between events quite like a ruler measures the distance between places; it does not measure the "speed" at which one instant follows another. Therefore the flow of time is evidently not objectively given, but a subjective phenomenon.

Time flow — a swindle?

This illusion cries out for an explanation, which, however, will not be found in physics, but in disciplines such as psychology, neurophysiology, and perhaps linguistics or cultural studies. Modern science has barely addressed the question of how we perceive the passing of time — here we can only speculate about the answer. Perhaps the problem hangs together with the functioning of the brain. When we spin around rapidly several times and suddenly stop, we get dizzy. Subjectively we have the impression that the world rotates around us, although our eyes see one and the same section. The apparent motion of the surroundings is an illusion produced by the rotation of the fluid in the inner ear. Perhaps the flow of time is something similar.

There are two aspects of the time asymmetry to which the false impression that time flows could be traced. The first is the thermodynamic distinction between past and future. As physicists have known for a few decades, the entropy of a system is closely connected to its information content. For this reason, the formation of memory is a directed process — new memories add information and lower the entropy of the brain. Perhaps we perceive this directedness as the flow of time.

A second possibility would be that our perception of the stream of time has something to do with quantum mechanics. Since the beginnings of this theory, physicists have been clear that time plays a unique role in it, quite different from space. Above all, the special role of time makes it so difficult to unite quantum mechanics with the general theory of relativity. Heisenberg's uncertainty relation, according to which nature itself is indeterministic, means that the future — and indeed also the past — is open. This indeterminism appears most clearly at atomic scales and says that the observable quantities characterizing a system vary from one moment to the next with a certain fuzziness.

For example, an electron striking an atom can bounce off in many different directions, and normally the result cannot be predicted exactly. The indeterminism of quantum physics has the consequence that for a quantum state there are many — possibly infinitely many — alternative futures, or possible realities. Quantum mechanics delivers relative probabilities for every observable outcome, but does not state which possible future will become actual.

When a human observer performs a measurement, however, he obtains a unique result; for example, he will find that the scattered electron moves in a particular direction. Through the measurement process, one single, unambiguous reality gets filtered out of a huge arsenal of possibilities. In the mind of the observer, the possible passes over into the actual, the open future into the fixed past — and that is exactly what we mean by the flow of time.

Physicists do not agree on how this transition from many possible realities to one single actuality happens. Many hold that it has something to do with the consciousness of the observer, because the act of observation forces nature to decide. Some researchers, like Roger Penrose of the University of Oxford, claim that consciousness — and the impression of temporal passage — hangs together with quantum processes in the brain.

Although so far there is no evidence of a special "time organ" in the brain corresponding, say, to the role of the visual cortex for visual perception, future research may identify the brain processes responsible for our sense of time. One could imagine agents that suspend the subjective impression of flowing time. Indeed, some people claim they can reach such mental states naturally through meditation.

What if science really could unmask the flow of time as a delusion? Perhaps we would no longer worry about the future or mourn the past. The fear of death would become as groundless as the fear of birth. Expectation and nostalgia might disappear from the human vocabulary. Above all, the sense of urgency that so often clings to our doings would pass. We would no longer be slaves to the drive to act right now, immediately, for present and future would literally be things of the past.

In brief


- Our senses tell us that time flows: the past is fixed, the future uncertain, and reality is experienced in the present. Yet various physical and philosophical arguments speak against it.

- The passing of time is probably an illusion. Perhaps consciousness involves thermodynamic or quantum-physical processes that produce the impression that we experience one instant after another.

The other dimension


The theologian Augustine wrote in the 5th century: "What, then, is time? If no one asks me, I know; if I want to explain it to someone who asks, I do not know." Because we experience time psychologically, physical definitions of time appear dry and inadequate to us. For the physicist, time is what clocks measure. Mathematically speaking, it is a one-dimensional space, ordinarily taken to be continuous. But it could also be quantized and consist of discrete "chronons" — like a film made of individual frames.

Time can be treated as the fourth dimension, but it is not identical with the three spatial dimensions. Both in everyday experience and in physical theory, time and space are treated differently. For example, the computation of distances in spacetime follows a different formula than ordinary spatial distances. The difference between space and time is closely tied to the concept of causality; it prevents cause and effect from hopelessly intermingling. On the other hand, many physicists believe that space and time lose their separate identity at the tiniest distances and time spans.

The present is nothing special


In everyday understanding, the present moment is unique: only now is the world really there. While the clock ticks, this moment passes and another arises. This process is what we call the flow of time. For example, the Moon occupies a certain position on its orbit around the Earth. A little later it is no longer at that place; instead it is found at another.

But natural scientists mostly hold that we cannot single out a special moment as present if everyone may claim that right. Objectively considered, past, present, and future are equally real. The whole of eternity exists in a four-dimensional block consisting of time and the three spatial dimensions.
